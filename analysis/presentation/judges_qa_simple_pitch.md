# Judge Q&A: the simplified DDD pitch

Prepared November 13, 2026 for **ddd_pitch_simple.pptx**. This guide addresses the **15-condo, gross-revenue proposal**, not the earlier 13-unit operating-income proposal. The presentation, allocation, and source tables have not been changed.

All currency is CAD. Answers in quotation marks are short responses to practise aloud. Use the follow-up material when asked. A strong answer distinguishes a verified result from an assumption and acknowledges missing evidence directly.

## Know these figures

| Item | Exact value | Spoken version |
| --- | ---: | --- |
| Purchase-price budget | $10,000,000 | Ten million dollars |
| Selected units | 15 | Fifteen condos |
| Estimated purchase spending | $9,873,090 | About $9.87 million |
| Unallocated purchase budget | $126,910 | About $127 thousand |
| Sum of selected segment-median annual revenues | $764,976 | About $765 thousand a year |
| Revenue / estimated purchase price | 7.7481% | About 7.75% **gross revenue yield** |
| Geography | Downtown Eastside, Downtown, Kitsilano | Three Vancouver neighbourhoods |

Allocation: Downtown Eastside has **7 one-bedroom condos**; Downtown has **3 one-bedroom and 2 two-bedroom condos**; Kitsilano has **3 one-bedroom condos**.

Do not describe $765,000 as profit, 7.75% as net return, or $9.87 million as a complete acquisition-and-launch budget.

## 1. “Is 7.75% our investment return?”

**Likely trigger:** Slides 1 and 4 put the ratio prominently beside an investment recommendation.

**Answer to practise**

> “It is gross annual estimated revenue divided by estimated purchase price. It tells us how much revenue each purchase dollar supports. It is not profit, net return, or IRR. We use it to screen and allocate properties; a final investment decision needs operating costs and total acquisition costs.”

**If asked why you omitted expenses:**

> “The brief does not provide property-level cost quotes, so we chose a transparent gross metric rather than imply precise net returns. But missing cost data does not make costs zero. Our gross recommendation is provisional until we test costs and confirm the total funding requirement.”

**Evidence:** `764,976 / 9,873,090 = 7.7481%`.

**Do not say:** “Costs are the same everywhere, so the ranking cannot change.” Proportional fees, fixed costs, strata differences, setup cash and integer purchases can change the allocation.

## 2. “Can you acquire and launch all 15 condos with $10 million?”

**Likely trigger:** Slides 1–2 say $9.87 million is spent, but leave only $127,000.

**Answer to practise**

> “Our current constraint covers purchase prices only. It does not establish that all fifteen units can be acquired, furnished and launched within ten million. Using the project's earlier setup assumptions, the same holdings would require about $10.64 million. We would need to reselect fewer or cheaper units within an all-in budget before treating this as executable.”

**Illustrative calculation for these exact 15 holdings:**

```text
Purchase price                         9,873,090.00
Acquisition costs: 4%                     394,923.60
Furniture: 15 × 25,000                    375,000.00
Total before additional working capital 10,643,013.60
Amount above the $10 million mandate      643,013.60
```

The 4% and $25,000 are project assumptions, not quotes. The shortfall already exceeds the entire unused purchase budget. Do not defend the 15-unit count as a verified all-in plan.

## 3. “What could profit look like after costs?”

**Answer to practise**

> “As a sensitivity, applying the project's existing cost assumptions to these exact fifteen holdings gives roughly $260,000 of annual operating income, before income tax and major capital works. That is illustrative, not our deck's headline and not a budget-feasible replacement recommendation, because the setup costs would push funding above ten million.”

**Illustrative assumptions and arithmetic:**

- Platform 15.5%, management 20%, cleaning/supplies 8%: **43.5% of revenue** in total.
- Annual condo strata $6,000, insurance $2,000, licence/registration $1,500 per unit.
- Property tax assumed at 0.3% of estimated purchase price.

```text
Estimated revenue                            764,976.00
Variable costs: 43.5%                        (332,764.56)
Fixed costs: 15 × 9,500 + 0.3% × 9,873,090   (172,119.27)
Illustrative annual operating income          260,092.17
```

Dividing by the illustrative $10.64 million setup cost gives about **2.44%**, rather than 7.75%. An additional reserve equal to 5% of revenue reduces the annual amount to about **$221,843**. Income tax, major capital works, financing and resale changes remain excluded. These calculations keep the same holdings and do not re-optimise the portfolio.

## 4. “Why use assessed values as acquisition prices?”

**Answer to practise**

> “They are public and allow consistent neighbourhood-level comparisons. We use assessed values as price proxies, not confirmed purchase prices. We join tax records to neighbourhoods and apply stated bedroom multipliers. Before acquisition, we would replace those proxies with property-specific price and condition evidence, especially for buildings that permit short-term rentals.”

**If challenged:** Consistency does not prove that the proxy is unbiased. Assessment-to-sale gaps and property mixes can differ by neighbourhood. The bedroom multipliers of 0.85, 1.15 and 1.5 are assumptions, not observed bedroom-specific prices.

**Prepare next:** Price sensitivity with reselection inside an all-in budget. Holding these same purchase quantities fixed at prices 20% higher would cost approximately **$11.85 million before setup**, so that fixed-holdings case would be infeasible.

## 5. “Why should historical revenue represent 2027?”

**Answer to practise**

> “We carry historical estimated annual revenue forward as a constant-revenue planning scenario. We have not fitted a 2027 forecasting model. Demand, supply, seasonality, events and a new property's ramp-up could change performance. Our result should therefore be presented with explicit revenue scenarios rather than as a guaranteed forecast.”

**If asked about event effects:** They are a possible risk to investigate, not a measured explanation established by this deck. Do not claim a quantified World Cup uplift without the analysis.

**Important distinction:** Adding segment medians produces a scenario total. It does not estimate the statistical median or expected value of the whole portfolio.

## 6. “What happens if revenue falls 20%?”

**Answer to practise**

> “Keeping the same holdings and purchase prices, a twenty-percent revenue reduction lowers annual gross revenue to about $612,000 and gross revenue yield to about 6.20%. Profit would fall more sharply in percentage terms because fixed costs remain. This is an assumed stress, not a probability estimate.”

**Illustrative operating-cost follow-up:** With the cost assumptions in question 3, operating income falls to approximately **$173,650**, before income tax and capital works. With a further reserve of 5% of stressed revenue, it is approximately **$143,051**. The acquisition funding gap still exists; a positive annual operating number does not solve that gap.

## 7. “Does 30 listings guarantee an accurate estimate?”

**Likely trigger:** Slides 3 and 8 use wording such as “trust” and “±14%”.

**Answer to practise**

> “No. Thirty is our screening threshold, informed by a sampling experiment. In one Downtown one-bedroom group, repeated samples of thirty listings had a median absolute relative deviation of about fourteen percent from the full group's median. Ninety percent of samples were within about thirty-four percent. That is not a ±14% confidence interval or a guarantee for other neighbourhoods.”

**What the experiment actually does:** It draws 5,000 random samples **without replacement** at each sample size and computes:

```text
Absolute relative deviation
  = abs(sample median / full observed-group median − 1)

Typical error = median of those deviations
90% within   = 90th percentile of those deviations
```

| Sample size | Typical absolute deviation | 90% of samples within |
| ---: | ---: | ---: |
| 20 | 17.4% | 40.9% |
| 30 | 14.2% | 33.9% |
| 50 | 10.5% | 25.9% |
| 100 | 7.5% | 16.2% |

The reference is the full **observed** group, not the true future population. This experiment does not measure errors in Inside Airbnb revenue estimates or purchase-price proxies.

## 8. “Why 30 rather than 20 or 50? Does that transfer to Kitsilano?”

**Answer to practise**

> “It is a practical compromise between retaining candidates and reducing sample instability. We do not claim that thirty is a universal statistical boundary. Kitsilano has only thirty-one comparables, so it is particularly sensitive to this rule. Testing other thresholds shows whether our recommendation depends on that choice.”

**Controlled comparisons using this deck's exact allocation rules:**

| Minimum listings | Units | Purchase spending | Annual gross revenue | Neighbourhoods |
| ---: | ---: | ---: | ---: | ---: |
| 20 | 15 | $9,985,123 | $776,886 | 4 |
| 30 | 15 | $9,873,090 | $764,976 | 3 |
| 50 | 16 | $9,891,535 | $752,375 | 3 |

At 20, West End is admitted; at 50, Kitsilano is excluded. These are heuristic reselections, not forecasts or proof of optimality. A stronger statistical validation would repeat the sampling experiment across segments rather than generalise one Downtown distribution.

## 9. “Why are one- and two-bedroom units within one point treated as tied?”

**Answer to practise**

> “The one-percentage-point gap is a business mixing rule, not a statistically established tie. Downtown one-bedroom and two-bedroom gross yields are close enough under that rule to alternate purchases. We accept a measured reduction in estimated revenue for a broader bedroom mix, but we have not demonstrated that the mix lowers portfolio risk.”

**Measured trade-off:** Buying only each neighbourhood's best eligible bedroom size gives **16 units**, about **$776,045** gross revenue, and **four neighbourhoods**. The deck's mix gives 15 units, $764,976, and three neighbourhoods. The gross-revenue reduction is **$11,069**, approximately **1.43% of the best-size-only comparison**.

**If asked whether that sacrifice is worthwhile:**

> “The revenue cost is measured; the diversification benefit is not. Without evidence on guest demand and correlated outcomes, we should present the mix as a preference and test it, rather than claim proven risk reduction.”

Do not equate the single-segment median sampling experiment with a test of the difference between price-adjusted yields. Also do not claim bedroom mixing improves geographical diversification: the best-size-only comparison actually spans more neighbourhoods here.

## 10. “Why cap a neighbourhood at 40%?”

**Answer to practise**

> “It is our concentration limit, not a rule imposed by the brief. With the cap, we buy fifteen units across three areas. Removing it under the same allocation method produces seventeen units and about $853,000 of gross revenue, but 73.5% of the total budget goes into one neighbourhood. We accept lower revenue to limit that exposure.”

| Rule | Units | Annual gross revenue | Largest area's share of full budget |
| --- | ---: | ---: | ---: |
| 40% cap | 15 | $764,976 | 36.8% |
| No neighbourhood cap | 17 | $852,572 | 73.5% |

The estimated gross-revenue difference is approximately **$87,596 per year**. Whole-unit purchases mean actual spending need not reach exactly 40%. The cap does not protect against citywide demand shocks, common condo risks or simultaneous building restrictions.

## 11. “You say every condo beats every house. Is that really true?”

**Answer to practise**

> “That statement applies to the eligible segment averages in our dataset, using at least thirty comparables, assessed-value prices and a gross-revenue metric. The lowest eligible condo segment is about 4.5%, versus about 3.0% for the best eligible house segment. It is not a claim about every individual property or every fully costed investment.”

**If asked about costs:** Condo strata fees, special assessments, repairs and short-term-rental restrictions could change the comparison. Screening gross revenue is useful, but it does not establish net superiority.

**If asked about excluded high-yield groups:** Very small groups may show higher gross yields but fail the sample threshold. Explain the rule consistently; do not hide excluded groups or claim that low sample size proves they are bad investments.

## 12. “Is your allocation optimal?”

**Answer to practise**

> “We use a transparent heuristic: rank eligible condo options, process neighbourhoods by their best gross yield, alternate qualifying bedroom sizes, and respect budget and supply limits. It produces a feasible purchase-price allocation. We have not solved all integer combinations or proved that it maximises total revenue.”

**If pressed:** The 16-unit best-size-only comparison already shows that the mixed allocation is not a maximum-revenue claim. An integer optimisation would need an explicit objective: gross revenue, operating income, or a risk-adjusted measure, plus acquisition/setup costs and the chosen limits.

## 13. “What are comparables? Can you actually buy those units?”

**Answer to practise**

> “Comparables are Airbnb listings grouped by neighbourhood, property type and bedroom count. They provide estimated operating performance, not an inventory of properties for sale. We have not verified fifteen available acquisition opportunities at the assumed prices or confirmed that the same performance can be replicated.”

**Follow-up:** The 10% segment limit is a team market-capacity assumption based on observed listing supply. It is not an availability check. Property size, quality, building rules and precise location still vary within each group.

## 14. “Are the bookings and revenues real observations?”

**Answer to practise**

> “The listing characteristics are observed in the public snapshot, but bookings, occupancy and annual revenue are Inside Airbnb estimates rather than verified transaction records. Reviews are a proxy for stays. We do not treat a calendar's unavailable nights as confirmed bookings because hosts can block dates.”

**Follow-up:** The 2026-labelled snapshot also contains mixed June/July scrape dates. Filtering known post-snapshot review dates reduces one date problem, but does not reconstruct every field as of June 15. Active-only selection can also be optimistic because it excludes less-active listings.

Do not call estimated occupancy, revenue and reviews three independent validations if they depend on the same estimation process.

## 15. “Does the proposed operation satisfy the rules?”

**Answer to practise**

> “The brief explicitly waives the principal-residence restriction for this simulation, so our investment-property analysis follows that instruction. It does not waive every operating rule. Licences, provincial registration, safety requirements and building or strata permissions still require confirmation.”

**Follow-up:** A licence-like number in listing text is not verified compliance. Do not display real licence numbers or identify hosts. No legal approval for the proposed properties has been established.

## 16. “How did you validate this, and what did AI do?”

**Answer to practise**

> “The headline and holdings can be traced to the allocation CSV and the script that generated it. We checked the total purchase spending and revenue, the budget and concentration rules, and the effect of the sample threshold and mixing rule. AI helped with code, analysis and presentation drafting. Those checks do not validate market acquisition prices or guarantee future revenue.”

**What you can demonstrate:**

- Allocation quantities sum to 15.
- Purchase spending sums to $9,873,090 and gross revenue to $764,976.
- Each neighbourhood is below $4 million of purchase spending.
- Each chosen segment has at least 30 listings and purchases no more than floor(10% of its sample).
- Changing the cap, sample threshold and bedroom-mixing gap produces the controlled comparisons above.

Each presenter should be able to explain the formula and one selected unit without saying only “the model chose it”. If a particular AI-use statement does not describe your team's actual work, adapt it rather than recite it.

## Details to correct or clarify before rehearsal

This guide does not edit the slides. These are issues to resolve when updating them:

1. **Gross versus net:** Label the headline 7.75% as gross revenue / purchase price, never net return.
2. **Funding feasibility:** Purchase prices fit $10 million; the same 15 units do not fit the earlier illustrative all-in setup assumptions.
3. **Sampling language:** Replace any interpretation of “±14% accuracy” with the measured absolute-deviation definition. The 90th-percentile deviation at 30 listings is about 34%.
4. **Reference-group count:** Slide 8 says 374 Downtown one-bedroom listings. The current allocation and listing-level filter use **365 valid-revenue observations**. Use the source population matching the experiment.
5. **Mixing claim:** “Within data noise” and “couples and families reduce risk” are not established by the current tests. The threshold is a business assumption.
6. **Data labels:** “Revenue, bookings” should make clear that these are estimated outcomes, not booking receipts.
7. **Uncertainty and actions:** The simplified main deck needs an explicit range/scenario and clear first actions before investment.
8. **AI disclosure:** Make material AI assistance and its checks visible, as required by the brief.

## How to rehearse

For a three-minute Q&A, practise each quoted answer in 20–30 seconds. First clarify the metric, then give one number or piece of evidence, then state the important limitation. Stop and allow the judge to follow up. Do not start with a long defence or imply that missing evidence has already been collected.

If you only prepare five answers, prioritise **gross versus net return, the all-in budget, assessed prices, the sampling-error interpretation, and the bedroom-mixing rule**.
